How the capture actually happens

Fair question, because most tools that say this mean "we indexed your wiki". Three concrete paths, none of which ask anyone to write a document:

OneSomeone says soComment @docbrain capture on a merge request. A webhook fires, DocBrain pulls the description and every human comment, extracts the Jira, Confluence and PR links inside them, and files the argument as a permanent cross-referenced record. That thread was never going to become a document.
TwoAn agent proposesOver MCP, a coding agent checks whether the organization already knows something, drafts what is missing, and asks a human to approve before it is recorded. This is the path that creates knowledge that existed nowhere.
ThreeThe gaps write themselvesQuestions nobody could answer are clustered, the gap behind them identified, and a draft written from what the record does hold. Routed to review, never published on its own.

Captured content is indexed through the same pipeline as everything else, so it is answerable and citable from the moment it lands.

Read-only, enforced

It cannot change your systems, by construction

Two gates, not a promise. A discovered tool is only registered if the upstream marks it read-only, and every static tool must declare the same. Then the dispatch layer refuses write operations regardless of what was registered, as a platform invariant.

So a write tool has to pass a registration gate it cannot pass, and then a dispatch gate that would reject it anyway. The OAuth scopes we ask for contain no write:, delete:, manage: or admin: at all.

The category, and where it goes next

Knowing what's true is the easy half.

Enterprise search is converging on the same idea: connect everything, answer from it. Everyone will have that. The half nobody puts at the centre is how your organization came to believe something, and whether it still should.

01

How you learned it

Not which document it sits in, but which conversation, ticket or change it came from, on every individual claim. You can open the thread someone actually argued it out in.

02

Why it's believed

Confidence, quality scoring and grounding on every answer, so a claim you can act on is visibly different from one you can't.

03

What failed before

Past incidents, prior attempts and corrections are part of the record. The thing you tried in 2023 that didn't work is knowledge too, usually the expensive kind.

04

Whether it's still valid

Freshness tracked continuously, contradictions surfaced with both origins, staleness cascaded to everything downstream. Old answers don't quietly stay wrong.

A search tool knows what your documents say. This knows what your organization has been through.

A note from the team building this

A product built on refusing to overclaim shouldn't overclaim about itself. So before you spend a week on a pilot, here is what we'd want to know if we were you.

The server is closed source. Everything runs inside your network, server included. Everything in our public repository is MIT and auditable — the CLI, the MCP server, the Helm charts, the configuration and the docs. The server ships as a container you run but cannot read the source of, and it is the component that touches your data. Self-hosting isn't unique to us. There are open-source alternatives and several are genuinely good at retrieval. One of them is more permissively licensed than we are. What they don't do is capture what was never written down. Judge us on that, not on deployment.

The questions you're about to ask

Answered before you have to send the email.

Q

We already have Confluence, Slack, Jira and GitHub. Why this?

Those are places to store knowledge somebody remembered to write down. DocBrain captures what never gets stored, the reasoning in a ticket, the fix in an incident thread, the decision made in a meeting, connects it across all of them and keeps it accurate. You're not replacing Confluence. You're capturing what Confluence never sees.

Q

Does anything leave our network? Do you train on our data?

No, and no. DocBrain runs entirely in your infrastructure and reads your systems without ever writing to them. Point it at a local model and nothing crosses your boundary at all. If you switch on learning, that happens inside your infrastructure too, on your hardware, on your data. We never see it, because there is no “we” in the path.

Q

What happens if we stop paying?

You keep running the version you have, in production, for as long as you like. You stop receiving updates, patches and support. Nothing is switched off, nothing phones home, and no data is held hostage, because it was never ours to hold.

Q

How is this different from enterprise AI search?

Search retrieves documents that already exist. If the answer was never written down, better search returns nothing, faster. DocBrain's job starts one step earlier, capturing the answer at the moment someone works it out, so there's something to retrieve at all.

Q

How long before it's useful?

It answers from your existing sources the day you connect them. Capture starts compounding immediately, and the value curve is the opposite of a wiki's. It gets better as your organization works, rather than decaying the moment someone stops maintaining it.

Q

Who shouldn't buy this?

If your documentation problem is that nobody reads a wiki that's actually accurate, you have a distribution problem and search will fix it more cheaply. If a closed-source server is disqualifying for you, that's a rational position and we'd rather say so now. And if you need a published accuracy benchmark to sign, we don't have one yet.
